\begin{abstract}
For $n$ integers and a target, exact subset sum has remained at the
meet-in-the-middle bound $\Oh^*(2^{n/2})$ for half a century in the worst case,
while the average-case representation technique now reaches
$\Ot(2^{0.291n})$ for hard knapsacks. This survey maps the field. We fix the
problem variants (subset sum, subset balancing over a coefficient set $C$,
Equal-Subset-Sum, Pigeonhole ESS, $k$-SUM, cardinality-constrained, dense,
modular), tabulate the classical and modern algorithms with their time, space,
model, and regime, and trace three distinct lines: worst-case exact algorithms,
average-case/cryptanalytic algorithms, and pseudopolynomial/dense algorithms.
We give a detailed account of the representation and dissection technique from
its origin through the recent worst-case results, state the remaining open
coefficient sets ($\{0,1\}$, $\{\pm1\}$, $\{\pm2\}$) precisely, collect the
conditional lower bounds that constrain them, and give a chronology. A companion
note reproduces the representation exponents from the primary sources. All
claims are sourced; reproduced values are marked as such.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{The problem}
Given integers $a_1,\dots,a_n$ and $b$, the \emph{exact subset sum} decision
problem asks whether some $x\in\{0,1\}^n$ satisfies
$\sum_{i=1}^n a_ix_i=b$; the search problem asks for a witness. Entries may be
negative, repeated values are distinct occurrences, and the empty subset is
allowed. Throughout, $\Oh^*(\cdot)$ suppresses factors polynomial in $n$ and in
the input bit length, and $W=\sum_i|a_i|$ is the magnitude sum. The difficulty
is a single constant: the pseudopolynomial and dense regimes are well
understood, but the leading exponential exponent for \emph{worst-case} exact
subset sum remains $1/2$.

\subsection{Why the exponent is subtle}
Three structural facts constrain every approach. (i) If
$|a_i|\le\mathrm{poly}(n)$ then $W\le\mathrm{poly}(n)$ and the dense dynamic
program is polynomial --- hardness needs large values. (ii) Completely large,
superincreasing values (e.g.\ powers of two) are also easy, because the target's
binary expansion is the witness. (iii) Hardness therefore concentrates near
density $d=n/\log_2\max_i|a_i|\approx1$, exactly where the cryptanalytic
representation technique lives. Powers of two also show that deduplicating
subset sums cannot force a better exponent: they produce $2^n$ distinct sums
while remaining easy.

\subsection{Variants}
Different variants behave very differently; Table~\ref{tab:variants} fixes
notation. A \emph{subset balancing} instance over a coefficient set
$C\subset\mathbb Z$ asks for a nonzero $c\in C^n$ with $\sum_i c_ia_i=0$; it
generalises subset sum and Partition. Densities are
$d=n/\log_2\max_i|a_i|$; the hard-knapsack regime is $d\approx1$.

\subsection{Scope and sources}
This survey compiles primary sources (arXiv, IACR ePrint, conference
proceedings) found in a structured literature pass, together with small

\section{Classical exact algorithms}\label{sec:classical}

\paragraph{Enumeration and dynamic programming.}
Enumerating all $2^n$ subsets is $\Oh(2^n)$. Bellman's dynamic program
(1957) runs in $\Oh(nt)$ time and space for target $t$ (equivalently $\Oh(nW)$
for the full range). Both are superseded exponentially; they matter because the
pseudopolynomial regime is where dense and small-target instances live
(Section~\ref{sec:pseudo}).

\paragraph{Meet-in-the-middle.}
Horowitz and Sahni~\cite{hs} split the entries into halves $L,R$, enumerate the
$2^{n/2}$ subset sums of each, sort one, and binary-search for complements
$u+v=b$. Cost: $\Oh^*(2^{n/2})$ time and space, deterministic, witness-producing.
A split-correctness lemma (a selection of $L\Vert R$ sums to $b$ iff there are
$u\in S(L)$, $v\in S(R)$ with $u+v=b$) makes it exact. This is the baseline.

\paragraph{Schroeppel--Shamir.}
Schroeppel and Shamir~\cite{ss} keep $\Oh^*(2^{n/2})$ time but reduce space to
$\Oh^*(2^{n/4})$. They split into four blocks of $n/4$, and for a guessed middle
value $\sigma_M$ enumerate the pair lists $\{\sigma_{12}\}$,
$\{\sigma_{34}\}$ matching modulo $2^{n/4}$, generating the needed pair sums
lazily in sorted order with two priority queues instead of materialising the
$2^{n/2}$ list. The general trade-off is roughly $T=\Oh(2^n/S^2)$ for space $S$.

\paragraph{Polynomial-space algorithms.}
Lokshtanov and Nederlof~\cite{ln} solve subset sum and knapsack in
pseudopolynomial time and polynomial space via algebraization
(inclusion--exclusion and M\"obius transforms over the DP table). Bansal, Garg,
Nederlof and Vyas~\cite{bgnv} give $\Oh^*(2^{0.86n})$ time in polynomial space.
These dominate on space, not time.

\section{Worst-case improvements below \texorpdfstring{$2^{n/2}$}{2\^{}n/2}}\label{sec:worstcase}

\paragraph{Polynomial-factor shaving.}
Chen, Jin, Randolph and Servedio~\cite{chen} gave the first improvement over
Horowitz--Sahni: $\Oh(2^{n/2}n^{-\gamma})$ for a constant $\gamma>0.5023$ in
standard word RAM or circuit RAM. The gain is polynomial, not a smaller base; it
combines the representation method with bit-packing
(Baran--Demaine--P\u{a}tra\c{s}cu).

\paragraph{Space versus time.}
Nederlof and W\k{e}grzycki~\cite{nw} reduce worst-case subset sum to the
Orthogonal Vectors problem, obtaining $\Oh^*(2^{0.5n})$ time and
$\Oh^*(2^{0.249999n})$ space (randomized), with a tight two-way relation between
the two problems. Belova, Chukhin, Kulikov and Mihajlin~\cite{bckm} lower the
space exponent to $0.246$. These improve space only.

\section{The representation and dissection technique}\label{sec:repr}

This is the engine behind the sub-$1/2$ exponents in the average case and,
recently, several worst-case cases.

\subsection{Two-part representation and the modulus filter}
Split a weight-$\ell\approx n/2$ solution as $x=y+z$ with $y,z$ disjoint, each of
weight $\approx\ell/2$. Each of the $\ell$ ones goes to $y$ or $z$, so $x$ has
$N=\binom{\ell}{\lfloor\ell/2\rfloor}\approx2^{\ell}$ representations. Choose a
modulus $M\approx N$ and a random residue $R$, and enumerate
$Y=\{y:a\cdot y\equiv R\pmod M\}$ and
$Z=\{z:a\cdot z\equiv b-R\pmod M\}$. A random $R$ keeps about one representation
of the plant; matching $Y$ and $Z$ exactly returns it. If the modular lists could
be built in time proportional to their size, the cost would be
\begin{equation}\label{eq:D}
D(\alpha)=\frac{2}{\alpha^{\alpha/2}(2-\alpha)^{(2-\alpha)/2}}\,2^{-\alpha},
\qquad \alpha=\ell/n,
\end{equation}
with $D(1/2)=2^{0.3113n}$ in the balanced case (HGJ~\cite{hgj}, Sec.~4.1). The
gap between $D(\alpha)$ and the achievable time is the crux.

\subsection{Four-way decomposition and unbalanced Schroeppel--Shamir}
The concrete HGJ algorithm~\cite{hgj} decomposes
$b=\sigma_1+\sigma_2+\sigma_3+\sigma_4$ into four weight-$(n/8)$ parts and solves
each modular sub-knapsack with the \emph{unbalanced} Schroeppel--Shamir
algorithm (a per-list construction costing $C(\alpha)=(\alpha^{-\alpha}
(1-\alpha)^{\alpha-1})^{1/2}$). With modulus $M\approx2^{\beta n}$,
$1/4<\beta<1/3$:
\begin{equation}
T(\beta)=\max\!\big(2^{0.3113n},\,2^{(0.0872+\beta)n}\big),\quad
M(\beta)=2^{(0.5436-\beta)n}.
\end{equation}
Table~\ref{tab:hgj} lists the trade-offs. May and Meurer corrected HGJ's claimed
$2^{0.3113n}$ to $2^{0.337n}$ by accounting for the pre-filter merge cost: when
two lists of size $L$ are merged with a modulus $M$, the intermediate list has
size $L^2/M$, and the complexity is $\max(L,L^2/M)$, not $\max(L,\tilde L)$. A
recursive variant (HGJ, Sec.~4.3) applies the decomposition to the complementary
knapsack near $\alpha=1/2$ and has thresholds $\alpha_0\approx0.312835$,
$\alpha_1\approx0.458959$.

\subsection{Generalised coefficient sets: BCJ}
Becker, Coron and Joux~\cite{bcj} extend the coefficient set from $\{0,1\}$ to
$\{-1,0,1\}$: a $1$ splits as $(0,1)$ or $(1,0)$, and a $0$ as $(0,0)$,
$(1,-1)$, or $(-1,1)$. Using three levels of decomposition with parameters
$\alpha,\beta,\gamma$:
\begin{align}
N_\nu(1)&=\tfrac14+\alpha, & N_\nu(-1)&=\alpha,\\
N_\kappa(1)&=\tfrac18+\tfrac\alpha2+\beta, & N_\kappa(-1)&=\tfrac\alpha2+\beta,\\
N_\omega(1)&=\tfrac1{16}+\tfrac\alpha4+\tfrac\beta2+\gamma, &
N_\omega(-1)&=\tfrac\alpha4+\tfrac\beta2+\gamma.
\end{align}
With moduli fixed by the single-decomposition condition, the list sizes are
$L_\chi=\mathrm{mult}(N_\chi)/\prod M$ and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bcj}
T=\max\!\Big(L_\omega,L_\omega^{1/2},\tfrac{L_\omega^2}{M_\kappa},
L_\kappa,\tfrac{L_\kappa^2}{M_\nu},L_\nu,
L_\nu^2\tfrac{M_\nu M_\kappa M_\omega}{2^n}\Big).
\end{equation}
Minimising gives $2^{0.291n}$ at $\alpha=0.0267$, $\beta=0.0168$,
$\gamma=0.0029$; at $\alpha=\beta=\gamma=0$ it recovers $0.337$.
Bonnetain, Bricout, Schrottenloher and Shen~\cite{bbss} use four-valued
representations to reach $2^{0.283n}$. These are \emph{average-case} bounds for
random hard knapsacks, resting on heuristic modular-distribution corollaries.

\subsection{Pseudo-solutions and compatibility certificates}
Increasing the number of coefficients per representation (e.g.\ allowing the
value $2$ or extra $\pm1$s) enlarges the solution-pair space so that a smaller
filtered list suffices, but creates \emph{pseudo-solutions}: pairs that satisfy
the sum/modular condition yet combine to an invalid coefficient vector. The
algorithm must find a \emph{compatible} pair, certified by a sampled set of
indices~\cite{nw,rw}. This ``compatibility certificate'' method is used for
Equal Subset Sum~\cite{rw} and underlies the worst-case results below.

\section{Worst-case representation: mixing and coefficient shifting}\label{sec:rw}

Randolph and W\k{e}grzycki~\cite{rw} move the representation technique to the
\emph{worst case} for a wide range of coefficient sets.

\paragraph{Mixing dichotomies.}
For random instances, solution representations ``mix well'' (their residues mod
$M$ are spread). For worst-case instances, they prove dichotomies: if solution
pairs do not mix well, the instance admits special structure (e.g.\ a small
doubling constant, or exponentially many solutions) solvable by subsampling or a
different algorithm; otherwise the generalised representation method applies.

\paragraph{Coefficient shifting.}
For coefficient sets without a $0$, they factor $C=C_1+C_2$ so that coefficients
acquire several representations, e.g.\
$\{0,1\}+\{-3,-2,1,2\}=[\pm3]$, where $\pm2$ has two representations.

\paragraph{Results and open cases.}
Table~\ref{tab:rw} lists the outcomes. The results leave exactly three
coefficient sets open: $\{0,1\}$ (Subset Sum), $\{\pm1\}$ (Partition), and
$\{\pm2\}$. We independently verified (Section~\ref{sec:frontier}) that these
are precisely the sets admitting \emph{no nontrivial sumset factorisation}, so
coefficient shifting has nothing to exploit.

\section{Where the target is already attained: related variants}\label{sec:variants}

\paragraph{Pigeonhole Equal Subset Sum.}
PESS (total sum $<2^n-1$, so a collision is guaranteed) is solved in
$\Oh^*(2^{n/3})$ by a randomized algorithm (Zhang, 2024; Jin--Williams--Zhang,
ESA 2025~\cite{jwz}), improving Jin--Wu's $\Oh^*(2^{0.4n})$~\cite{jw}. The key
structural quantity is $F=\sum_t\max(0,\mathrm{cnt}(t)-1)$, the number of
colliding subset-sum pairs. Small $F$ forces the weights to be close to the
geometric progression $1,2,4,\dots$, collapsing PESS to a small ESS instance on
a few low-order elements; large $F$ means many solutions, handleable by
subsampling and mod-$p$ dynamic programming. A polynomial-space variant runs in
$\Oh^*(2^{2n/3})$ time. A FOCS 2026 line gives a deterministic dense algorithm
$\mathrm{poly}(n)M^{o(1)}$ ($M=2^{o(n)}$), an average-case
$\mathrm{poly}(n)M^{1/4}$, and a lattice-based conditional lower bound.

\paragraph{Equal Subset Sum.}
Worst-case ESS is at $\Oh^*(1.6994^n)$ (Yamano--Shibuya, 2026)~\cite{ys},
improving $1.7067^n$~\cite{rw} and $1.7088^n$ (MNPW, ESA 2019); the baseline is
$3^{n/2}=1.7321^n$.

\paragraph{Either-Or Subset Sum.}
A hybrid returning either a subset-sum witness or a pair of equal-sum subsets is
solvable in randomized worst-case $\Oh^*(2^{0.461n})$ (Randolph's
thesis~\cite{rand}), the strongest concrete evidence toward breaking the
meet-in-the-middle barrier for $\{0,1\}$ itself.

\paragraph{Quantum algorithms.}
Bernstein, Jeffery, Lange and Meurer~\cite{bjlm} solve worst-case subset sum in
$\Oh^*(2^{n/3})$; Chukhin, Kulikov, Levitskii and Mihajlin~\cite{cklm} give
$\Oh^*(2^{2n/7})\approx2^{0.2857n}$ via a worst-case quantum $k$-SUM algorithm
with exponent $\Psi_k=2k/7+\Oh(1)$. Hence $1/3$ is not
information-theoretically special; the classical obstruction is algorithmic.

\paragraph{Cardinality-constrained.}
Fixing the Hamming weight of the solution turns the problem into $k$-SUM, where
meet-in-the-middle is $\Oh(n^{\lceil k/2\rceil})$ and the $k$-SUM conjecture
asserts this is essentially optimal. In the low-weight cryptanalytic regime,
Esser and May (EUROCRYPT 2020) give a heuristic $2^{0.645n}$ algorithm
(improving BCJ's $2^{0.72n}$ random-subset-sum bound); it needs many solutions
and is not worst-case.

\paragraph{Proximity and integer programming.}
Eisenbrand and Weismantel's Steinitz-lemma proximity gives
$(m\Delta)^{\Oh(m)}\mathrm{poly}$ algorithms for integer programs; Randolph and
Węgrzycki (ESA 2024) prove an exact equivalence between bounded-doubling subset
sum and hyperplane-constrained binary ILP, recasting subset-sum hardness as ILP
dimension/additive structure. For vanilla $\{0,1\}$ ($m=1$) this yields only a
pseudopolynomial bound.

\section{Dissection, generalised birthday, and cryptanalysis}\label{sec:crypto}

\paragraph{Generalised birthday.}
Wagner's $k$-tree algorithm~\cite{wag} solves the modular $k$-sum in
$\Oh(k\cdot2^{n/(1+\log k)})$; for $k=4$ this is $2^{n/3}$. Lyubashevsky (2005)
and Shallue (2008) apply it to Random Modular Subset Sum with modulus
$M=2^{n^\epsilon}$, giving $2^{\Oh(n^\epsilon/\log n)}$ bounds. These are
average-case results for dense/many-solution instances; the representation
technique is the low-density counterpart.

\paragraph{Lattice reduction.}
Lagarias and Odlyzko~\cite{lo} solve almost all random knapsacks of density
$d<0.6463$ in polynomial time given an SVP oracle; Coster et al.\ extend this to
$d<0.9408$. BKZ-based attacks dominate the density-$\approx1$ regime. Joux and
Węgrzycki (STACS 2026) improve the modular approach, roughly doubling the
admissible density. Lattice methods are average-case/heuristic and orthogonal to
the worst-case target.

\section{Additive combinatorics and the density dichotomy}\label{sec:additive}

Classical results (S\'ark\"ozy, Alon--Kleitman, Szemer\'edi--Vu) describe how
long arithmetic progressions and structured sumsets force dense subset sums to
behave well. A recurring theme is a \emph{dichotomy}: either the instance has
additive structure (small sumset, small doubling, few representations) and is
easy, or it has many representations and the representation/subsampling
machinery applies. The sub-$1/2$ worst-case results~\cite{rw} are built on such
dichotomies; the coefficient sets without a $0$ resist them. We probed two
folklore hypotheses with small experiments: dissociativity ($F=0$) does not make
an instance hard (representations remain modularly spread), and pseudo-solution
load is concentrated in the \emph{easy} collision-heavy families, not in the
hard-looking dissociated ones.

\section{The frontier and open problems}\label{sec:frontier}

We independently searched sumset factorisations $C=C_1+C_2$ and found that the
three open coefficient sets are exactly those with no nontrivial factorisation
(every coefficient keeps one representation), whereas $\{-1,0,1\}$ and
$\{\pm3\}$ acquire multiples (Table~\ref{tab:shift}).

\paragraph{Open problems.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Is there an $\Oh^*(2^{(1/2-\epsilon)n})$ worst-case algorithm for
$\{0,1\}$ subset sum? The equivalent open cases are Partition and $\{\pm2\}$.
\item Prove or refute the Meet-in-the-Middle conjecture; Either-Or Subset Sum is
the strongest evidence against it.
\item Close the average-case/worst-case gap for $\{0,1\}$: find a worst-case
mechanism that does not need a sumset factorisation, or a conditional lower
bound.
\item Optimise space constants across the worst-case results
($\Oh^*(2^{0.246n})$ space; poly-space exponents).
\end{enumerate}
