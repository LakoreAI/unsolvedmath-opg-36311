\title{The Representation Technique for Exact Subset Sum:\\
Reproducing the $2^{n/2}$-to-$2^{0.291n}$ Frontier}
\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Reproducible Research Note}}
\begin{document}
\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
The exact subset sum problem has a classical worst-case $\Oh^*(2^{n/2})$
meet-in-the-middle algorithm. The representation/dissection technique of
Howgrave-Graham--Joux and Becker--Coron--Joux lowers the exponent to
$\tilde{\Oh}(2^{0.291n})$ for \emph{random} hard knapsacks, but not in the
worst case. We reproduce the complexity models of both papers from their
primary sources and numerically recover their published optima: the HGJ
two-part ideal $2^{0.3113n}$, the HGJ simple algorithm $2^{0.337n}$
(May--Meurer corrected), and the BCJ three-level optimum $2^{0.291n}$. We then
probe \emph{where} the technique stalls: generic and dissociated instances have
well-spread modular representations and no pseudo-solutions, while the open
coefficient sets $\{0,1\}$, $\{\pm1\}$, $\{\pm2\}$ are exactly those that admit
no nontrivial sumset factorisation, so the worst-case tool of coefficient
shifting cannot be applied. No worst-case improvement is claimed.
\end{abstract}
\begin{IEEEkeywords}
subset sum, representation technique, meet-in-the-middle, dissection, hard
knapsacks, reproducibility
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
Given integers $a_1,\dots,a_n,b$, the exact subset sum problem asks whether some
$x\in\{0,1\}^n$ satisfies $\sum_i a_ix_i=b$. Horowitz--Sahni solve it in
$\Oh^*(2^{n/2})$ time and space~\cite{hs}; Schroeppel--Shamir keep the
$\Oh^*(2^{n/2})$ time with $\Oh^*(2^{n/4})$ space~\cite{ss}. Whether the
exponent can be reduced below $1/2$ in the worst case is open. For \emph{random}
hard knapsacks of density $d=n/\log_2\max_i a_i\approx1$, the representation
technique of Howgrave-Graham--Joux~\cite{hgj} and Becker--Coron--Joux~\cite{bcj}
reaches $\tilde{\Oh}(2^{0.337n})$ and $\tilde{\Oh}(2^{0.291n})$ respectively;
Chen et al.~\cite{chen} give the first worst-case improvement, but only a
polynomial factor $2^{n/2}/n^{-0.5023}$. Most recently,
Randolph--Węgrzycki~\cite{rw} break the meet-in-the-middle barrier in the
\emph{worst case} for most constant coefficient sets, leaving
$\{0,1\}$, $\{\pm1\}$ (Partition), and $\{\pm2\}$ open.

This note does three things. It reproduces the HGJ and BCJ complexity models
and their published optima from the primary sources (Sections~\ref{sec:hgj}
and~\ref{sec:bcj}); it probes which structural hypotheses about the barrier
hold (Section~\ref{sec:probes}); and it characterises the open cases
(Section~\ref{sec:frontier}). It makes no worst-case claim.

\section{The representation principle}\label{sec:principle}
Meet-in-the-middle splits the $n$ entries into two halves $L,R$ and looks for
$u\in S(L),v\in S(R)$ with $u+v=b$ (Lemma of split correctness), at cost
$\Oh^*(2^{n/2})$. The representation technique instead represents a solution
$x\in\{0,1\}^n$ in many ways as a sum of partial vectors, so that a random
modular filter keeps about one representation while shrinking the search.

For a solution of Hamming weight $\ell\approx n/2$, write
$b=\sigma_1+\sigma_2$ with $\sigma_i$ a sum of $\lceil\ell/2\rceil$ or
$\lfloor\ell/2\rfloor$ entries. The number of such decompositions is
$N=\binom{\ell}{\lfloor\ell/2\rfloor}\approx2^{\ell}$. Choose a modulus
$M\approx N$ and a random $R$; heuristically some decomposition has
$\sigma_1\equiv R$ and $\sigma_2\equiv b-R\pmod M$. Constructing the two
subsets of solutions and matching them costs
\begin{equation}\label{eq:two-part}
D(\alpha)=\frac{2}{\alpha^{\alpha/2}(2-\alpha)^{(2-\alpha)/2}}\cdot 2^{-\alpha},
\end{equation}
where $\alpha=\ell/n$; in the balanced case $D(1/2)=\tilde{\Oh}(2^{0.3113n})$.
The remaining difficulty is to construct the modular solution lists in time
proportional to their size; this is the crux.

\subsection{Generalised coefficient sets}
Becker--Coron--Joux extend the coefficient set from $\{0,1\}$ to
$\{-1,0,1\}$: a $1$ is split as $(0,1)$ or $(1,0)$, and a $0$ as $(0,0)$,
$(1,-1)$, or $(-1,1)$. With a parameter $\alpha$ giving $(1/4+\alpha)n$ ones and
$\alpha n$ minus-ones per side, the number of representations is
$N_D=\binom{n/2}{n/4}\cdot\binom{n/2}{\alpha n,\alpha n,(1/2-2\alpha)n}$.

\section{Reproducing the HGJ exponents}\label{sec:hgj}
The HGJ simple algorithm~\cite{hgj} decomposes $b$ into four parts
$b=\sigma_1+\sigma_2+\sigma_3+\sigma_4$, each a weight-$(n/8)$ sum, and solves
each sub-knapsack with the \emph{unbalanced} Schroeppel--Shamir algorithm. With
modulus $M\approx2^{\beta n}$, $1/4<\beta<1/3$, the paper's analysis gives
\begin{equation}
T(\beta)=\max(2^{0.3113n},\,2^{(0.0872+\beta)n}),\qquad
M(\beta)=2^{(0.5436-\beta)n}.
\end{equation}
The optimum is at $\beta\to1/4$: time $2^{0.338n}$ with $2^{0.294n}$ memory.
Other trade-offs are $\beta\to1/3$ ($2^{0.421n}$ time, $2^{0.211n}$ memory) and
$\beta\approx0.2936$ (Schroeppel--Shamir memory $2^{n/4}$, time $2^{0.381n}$).
The recursive variant (HGJ, Sec.~4.3)~\cite{hgj} applies the decomposition to the
complementary knapsack near $\alpha=1/2$; its thresholds are
$\alpha_0\approx0.312835$ and $\alpha_1\approx0.458959$.
Table~\ref{tab:repro} shows these values reproduced numerically.

\section{Reproducing the BCJ exponents}\label{sec:bcj}
BCJ~\cite{bcj} use three levels of $\{-1,0,1\}$ decomposition with parameters
$\alpha,\beta,\gamma$. The coefficient counts are
\begin{align}
N_\nu(1)&=\tfrac14+\alpha, & N_\nu(-1)&=\alpha,\\
N_\kappa(1)&=\tfrac18+\tfrac\alpha2+\beta, & N_\kappa(-1)&=\tfrac\alpha2+\beta,\\
N_\omega(1)&=\tfrac1{16}+\tfrac\alpha4+\tfrac\beta2+\gamma, &
N_\omega(-1)&=\tfrac\alpha4+\tfrac\beta2+\gamma.
\end{align}
With the moduli fixed by the ``single decomposition'' conditions, the list sizes
are $L_\chi=\mathrm{mult}(N_\chi)/\prod M$, and the running time is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bcj}
T=\max\!\big(L_\omega,L_\omega^{1/2},\tfrac{L_\omega^2}{M_\kappa},
L_\kappa,\tfrac{L_\kappa^2}{M_\nu},L_\nu,
L_\nu^2\tfrac{M_\nu M_\kappa M_\omega}{2^n}\big).
\end{equation}
Minimising \eqref{eq:bcj} numerically reproduces BCJ's optimum at
$\alpha=0.0267,\ \beta=0.0168,\ \gamma=0.0029$, giving $T=2^{0.291n}$; at
$\alpha=\beta=\gamma=0$ it recovers the May--Meurer-corrected HGJ value
$2^{0.337n}$.

\section{Structural probes}\label{sec:probes}
We implemented a weak (balanced) variant of the HGJ search and probed the
structural hypotheses that have been proposed for the barrier.

\paragraph{Modular coverage.} For a planted solution, the residues
$a\cdot y\bmod M$ of its $C(n/2,n/4)$ representations, with $M$ near the
representation count, are \emph{spread} at the random-birthday level
(coverage $\approx0.6$) for dissociated, density-1, and near-geometric
families; only deliberately concentrated families (arithmetic, equal weights)
are low, and they are easy. Dissociativity is therefore \emph{not} the barrier.

\paragraph{Pseudo-solutions.} Resolving target representations by cardinality,
dissociated, geometric, and near-geometric instances have a unique
weight-$n/2$ solution and \emph{zero} smaller-cardinality representations
(i.e.\ no pseudo-solution load); pseudo-solutions appear only in the
collision-heavy easy families. Pseudo-solution blow-up is therefore also
\emph{not} the barrier.

\section{The open cases}\label{sec:frontier}
Randolph--Węgrzycki's worst-case tool is \emph{coefficient shifting}: a sumset
factorisation $C=C_1+C_2$ in which coefficients acquire multiple
representations, e.g.\ $\{0,1\}+\{-3,-2,1,2\}=[\pm3]$, where $\pm2$ has two
representations. Searching factorisations $C_1,C_2\subseteq[-3,3]$
(Table~\ref{tab:shift}), the open cases $\{0,1\}$, $\{\pm1\}$
(Partition), and $\{\pm2\}$ are \emph{exactly} those with no nontrivial
factorisation: every coefficient keeps a single representation. Sets with a $0$
coefficient ($\{-1,0,1\}$) or a nontrivial factorisation ($\{\pm3\}$) obtain
the surplus that shifting needs.

This independently reproduces the frontier: the obstacle for $\{0,1\}$ is the
absence of a nontrivial sumset factorisation (equivalently, of a $0$
coefficient), so the worst-case representation technique has no surplus to
exploit. A worst-case $2^{n/3}$ algorithm for $\{0,1\}$ must therefore use a
\emph{different} mechanism.

\section{Reproducibility}
All exponents are produced by small dependency-free Python scripts:
\texttt{representation\_exponents.py} (Table~\ref{tab:repro},
Figure~\ref{fig:d}), \texttt{coefficient\_shifting.py} (Table~\ref{tab:shift}),
and the probe
scripts \texttt{modular\_structure.py} and \texttt{pseudo\_solutions.py}. Each
writes a Markdown report to \texttt{docs/analysis/}. The Lean correctness layer
and the baseline implementations are unchanged from the companion note. The
figures are regenerated with \texttt{uv run python
scripts/analysis/plot\_representation.py}.

\section{Conclusion}
The representation technique already reaches $2^{0.291n}$ for random hard
knapsacks. Its limits are not a sub-solver or a class of hard-looking
instances (those are representation-friendly), but the average-case nature of
its modular guarantees and the lack of coefficient shifting for $\{0,1\}$,
$\{\pm1\}$, $\{\pm2\}$. A worst-case $2^{n/3}$ bound for exact subset sum
requires a new mechanism for these sets, or a conditional lower bound.
